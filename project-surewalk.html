Sure Walk App

Sure Walk is a student-run safety service at the University of Texas at Austin that provides late-night rides to help students, faculty, and staff get home safely. Currently, the service is requested through a Google Form rather than a dedicated app. Our team explored how a mobile app could better support the Sure Walk experience by addressing usability, communication, and coordination challenges for both riders and employees.

Challenge

The Sure Walk experience involves multiple parties, from riders, drivers, navigators, and dispatchers, each with different goals and pain points. The challenge was to understand where friction occurred across these roles, particularly in areas such as awareness, communication, and task flow, and to prioritize features that would meaningfully improve safety, usability, and operational efficiency within a single app experience. The stakes rise with demand: on rainy nights Sure Walk can get 200–300 ride requests, all coordinated by dispatchers through a Google Sheet and a group chat.

Outcome

The project is ongoing and the app hasn’t launched yet. We’ve tested the first round of high-fidelity rider designs with 3 sponsored users, and we’re now working on an admin dashboard for Sure Walk staff, with a goal of launching by the end of the Fall 2026 semester. So far, the research has changed what we’re building first. Instead of polishing the existing request form, we’re focusing on real-time wait-time visibility and clearer status updates, so riders know their request was received and when to expect pickup.

Research scope: a survey of UT students and Sure Walk staff (32 responses), 7 interviews with riders, drivers, navigators, and staff, a Service Safari where our team booked and rode Sure Walk ourselves, observations of dispatchers during live shifts, and usability tests with 3 sponsored users.

Process

We surveyed UT students and current or former Sure Walk staff to understand usage patterns, awareness, and pain points across roles. Using affinity diagramming, we synthesized insights and developed “How might we” questions to guide feature prioritization. These insights informed early sketches and low-fidelity wireframes.

Grounding Design in Rider and Staff Needs

By engaging UT students alongside current and former Sure Walk staff, including riders, drivers, navigators, dispatchers, and non-users, we identified early breakdowns in awareness and usability that were affecting how people accessed and coordinated the service. These insights surfaced consistent confusion around how requests were submitted and tracked, especially through the Google Form system. This directly shaped our decision to prioritize clarity in request flow and end-to-end visibility across roles, rather than focusing on isolated UI improvements.

Mapping Needs Across Every Role

Because a single ride depends on several people doing their part, we looked at what each role needed from the system rather than designing for riders alone. This kept us from solving one group's problem by creating a new one for another.

Riders

Need confirmation that their request went through, an honest sense of when pickup will happen, and a heads-up before anything changes.

Drivers & Navigators

Need accurate pickup details and a reliable way to reach riders, so a missed connection doesn't turn into a cancelled ride.

Dispatchers

Need one shared view of incoming requests and ride status, without relaying information through side channels.

Non-Users

Need to know Sure Walk exists, who it's for, and how to request a ride before they're in a moment where they need one.

Uncovering Systemic Issues Across the Service

Through affinity diagramming, we synthesized responses from all user groups and uncovered recurring breakdowns in communication, workflow coordination, and service transparency. This revealed a trust gap in the system's feedback loop, specifically around wait time transparency. It directly influenced our decision to prioritize real-time wait-time visibility and clearer status updates, ensuring riders could understand not just whether their request was received, but when they could actually expect pickup.

Connecting Insights to Design Priorities

Each priority traces back to a specific finding, which gave the team a shared rationale when deciding what to build first.

  • Riders couldn't tell when pickup would happen → real-time wait estimates and ride status.
  • Requests were cancelled without warning → proactive notifications before and after a ride changes.
  • Staff relied on side channels → a shared request view connecting dispatchers, drivers, and navigators.
  • Many students had never heard of the service → a clear, discoverable entry point in place of a buried form link.

Content writing: the Sure Walk voice

Riders open Sure Walk late at night, often alone, and often anxious. The research showed that the biggest trust gap wasn’t a missing feature, it was silence: riders had “no idea when the driver will arrive,” and one rider’s request was cancelled without any notification. I wrote a tone-of-voice guide so every message in the app closes that gap by telling riders what is happening and what happens next.

Three Voice Principles

Transparent

Always explain changes, including cancellations, and tell riders about the next step in the ride early.

Clear

Give real time estimates instead of vague updates, so riders know how long they’ll wait.

Calm, not alarming

State facts steadily, even when a ride is delayed or cancelled.

Adjusting Tone to Each Moment of the Ride

The voice stays the same, but the tone shifts with what the rider is going through. Each moment has its own goal.

RequestingBrief but guided

Where are you headed?

WaitingSpecific, reassuring, time-bound

Your ride is estimated to arrive in 7 minutes.

ArrivalStraightforward, time-bound

Your driver has arrived, you have 2 minutes.

CancellationDirect and honest

Your ride was cancelled. Would you like to request another ride?

After hoursStraightforward and guided

The service is currently unavailable as it is outside of hours. We open at [time] today.

Choosing Words Riders Already Know

I aligned terminology with Parking & Transportation Services’ existing materials where it helped, and swapped in plainer words where it didn’t, so the app reads easily on a phone screen.

Writing Rules for Consistency

  • Active voice, present tense: “Your driver is on the way.”
  • Buttons start with a verb, use sentence case, and have no punctuation: “Request a ride.”
  • Times are consistent: “12:30 a.m.” for clock times, “8 min” for waits, and estimates are always labeled as estimates.
  • No exclamation points or emoji in status or safety messages.
  • Errors say what happened, then what to do, and never blame the rider.

Prioritization & tradeoffs

  • Built first: the rider flow (requesting a ride, seeing the wait time, and getting status updates), because that’s where the research showed trust breaking down.
  • Next: an admin dashboard for Sure Walk staff, which we’re designing now that the rider side has been tested. We mapped staff needs early so the dashboard builds on that research.
  • Why: Parking & Transportation Services, our client, made the rider experience its main focus, and riders were the group feeling the most visible pain in our research.
  • Working with engineering: I partnered with engineers to document specs, interaction states, and edge cases, and adjusted designs to fit technical constraints.

Conclusion

This project highlighted the value of multi-stakeholder research for safety-critical services like Sure Walk. By engaging both riders and employees, we uncovered shared and role-specific pain points that helped ground design decisions in real operational needs. The app hasn’t launched yet, but these insights shaped the rider experience we’ve tested and now the staff admin dashboard we’re designing. Our goal is to launch by the end of the Fall 2026 semester.
